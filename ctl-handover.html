Where it stands, 22 September

Since this document was first written, four of the systems it describes have been switched on and tested end to end. They are running on a preview address — the same site, at a temporary web address, so it can be checked before ctlpro.com points at it.

The contact form sends. An enquiry from the site arrives in the office inbox, and the reply address is the customer’s, so answering the notification answers them. Tested both ways: it was also deliberately broken, to confirm that a visitor is told to phone instead of being shown a false “thanks, we got that” over a lost enquiry.

The gallery is editable without us. The forty photographs now come from an online editor your office signs into by email invitation. Photographs are uploaded at full size and resized automatically for the site, so a phone photo no longer costs your visitors a slow page.

Every enquiry is written down. A second copy of each one is recorded in a database of ours, separately from the email, and can be exported as a spreadsheet at any time. This is the part that matters most and the part most contractor sites do not have: if an email is deleted, misfiled, or sent to somebody who has left, the enquiry still exists.

Nothing is lost while the CRM question is open. Enquiries are being collected now and marked as not-yet-sent-anywhere. The moment a customer system is chosen, every one collected in the meantime is delivered to it automatically. Choosing late costs nothing; that was the point of building it this way.

What is not on yet, and why. Live Google review stars need your API key (item 09). Job applications need a decision on where résumés go (item 10). The legal pages need your attorney’s eyes (item 27). The customer system needs picking (item 12). None of these stop the site working; each is a decision or a login rather than more building.

Three things to start before anything else

Almost everything on the list can be done in the room. These three cannot — each one involves somebody outside it, and each takes days. Starting them at the top of the session means they run in the background while the rest gets worked through.

1. The attorney. Four documents need a read: the terms, the privacy policy, the six questions on the job application, and the language on the storm page about how CTL works a claim. That last one is the important one — in Louisiana, negotiating a claim on a homeowner's behalf is public adjusting and needs a licence. The page is written so CTL documents the damage, meets the adjuster and scopes the repair, and the homeowner files and negotiates. If that is not exactly how you operate, the wording has to change.

2. The Google Business Profile. Confirm who owns it. If nobody at CTL knows, the reclaim process starts today — Google takes days to a couple of weeks, and it sits on the critical path for the reviews on the site.

3. The old site's address book. We need a list of every page on the current ctlpro.com before it comes down, plus a copy of its DNS settings. Both are only obtainable while it is still running. This one is ours to do — we just need to know who hosts it.

Block A — Accounts, logins and keys

The unglamorous half of a launch, and the half that decides whether CTL owns its own website afterwards. Every account below should be in your name with us added to it, not the other way around.

  1. 01

    The domain registrar login for ctlpro.com

    Wherever the domain itself was bought. We need to confirm it is unlocked and that the transfer code can be retrieved.

  2. 02 Blocks the launch

    The current DNS settings, and a list of the current site’s pages

    Both from whoever hosts the site today. The settings are usually a one-click export; the page list we can gather ourselves once we know where to look.

    Two reasons, and the first is the serious one — the same settings that point ctlpro.com at a website also point your email at your mailbox. Moving the site without copying those across first stops company email arriving. Not slows it. Stops it. The page list is the other half: every address Google has indexed and every link posted to Facebook needs somewhere to land on the new site, or it starts returning “not found” the day we switch. Both jobs are only possible while the old site is still up.

  3. 03

    A Cloudflare account in CTL’s name

    This is where the site will be hosted. Created under CTL’s email with us added as a member — so if we ever part ways, the site does not leave with us. Free at the traffic a regional contractor sees.

    This one got more urgent, not less. Everything built so far runs on a personal account of ours, because it had to run somewhere to be testable — and that account now carries a card on file for the file storage the job applications need. None of it is difficult to move, but all of it has to move, and the longer the list of things living there grows, the more there is to migrate. Doing this early in the session is the single cheapest item on the page.

  4. 04

    Names and email addresses for whoever edits the site

    Whoever will add gallery photos gets invited to the photo editor by email. No account to create beforehand and nothing to install — they click the invitation and set a password.

    This got simpler. The first version of the editor would have needed each of them to hold a developer account, which is a strange thing to ask of an office. That was replaced with one that invites people by email instead, so all we need from you is the list.

  5. 05 Start today

    Ownership of the Google Business Profile

    The listing that shows up in Maps and in the sidebar when somebody searches “roofer Lafayette”. We need manager access to point it at the new site and to pull your reviews in.

    Your stars in search come from here, not from the website — which is also why the site does not mark up its own reviews. See “things left off on purpose” below.

  6. 06

    Google Search Console

    Free, and it is how we tell Google the new site exists and watch for anything broken afterwards. Created against the same Google account as the Business Profile.

  7. 07

    Who hosts the site now, and when does that bill end

    Do not cancel it on launch day.

    Keep it running 30 days past the switch. If anything goes wrong, going back is a five-minute change — but only while the old site still exists to go back to.

  8. 08 Done — 22 September

    A form key for the office inbox

    Settled. The key exists and the form sends; an enquiry submitted on the preview site arrives in the inbox, with the customer’s address as the reply-to. Nothing further is needed from you on this item.

    It was also tested the other way round, with the key deliberately wrong, to confirm the visitor is told to phone rather than shown a false “thanks, we got that” over an enquiry that went nowhere.

    One thing to confirm in the room: the inbox it sends to is the one the office actually watches, and ideally a shared address rather than one person’s, so leads do not sit unread when somebody is out or leaves.

    Why it was built to fail loudly — the alternative is a form that looks like it worked and quietly loses the lead. That is the behaviour that was tested, and it is why this item was the one that had to be cleared first.

  9. 09

    A Google API key and your place ID

    Turns the live Google reviews on, so the rating shown is the one you actually have that morning. The key gets locked to your domain so nobody else can spend against it.

    Worth knowing — this is the one thing on the site that costs money per visitor, so we set a spending alert the same day it is switched on. The place ID also gets checked against your real listing before it goes anywhere: the wrong one would show a different business’s reviews under your name.

Block B — Decisions only you can make

Seven choices. Each one switches on something that is already built and currently sitting dark. None needs research beforehand — they are questions about how CTL actually works.

  1. 10 Blocks the launch

    Where do résumés go?

    The careers page is live in the menu and the application form is built and tested. What is not settled is where the CVs land. As it stands that would be private storage we run — which works, but it means we are holding strangers’ personal documents on CTL’s behalf. For a company your size that responsibility is better sitting with a company whose actual business is holding them.

    Until this is settled the form tells applicants to email the office instead. That is deliberate — a form that appears to take somebody’s CV and quietly drops it costs them a job they think they applied for, and they never find out.

  2. 11

    What do you use to track jobs and customers?

    JobNimbus, AccuLynx, Roofr, Leap — or a whiteboard and a phone. There is no wrong answer; we just need to know, because the site already collects every enquiry into one list and can push it straight into whatever you use.

    If the answer is “nothing yet” that is fine and nothing is lost. Enquiries are stored from day one, and the first time a system is connected, everything collected up to that point gets delivered into it.

  3. 12

    Which inbox gets leads — and who is the second pair of eyes?

    Every assessment request emails somebody. We need the address, and we want a second one on it.

    One mailbox is a single point of failure. The most common way a website loses a job is that the enquiry arrived correctly and nobody opened it that week.

  4. 13

    Do you want to know which ads are producing calls?

    Call tracking gives a different phone number to the website, Facebook and Google so you can see which one rings. It costs a monthly fee and it is genuinely useful — or we skip it and every number on the site stays your real one, which is what it is today.

  5. 14

    Which visitor statistics do you want?

    Two choices. A paid one that does not track people across the internet and keeps the consent banner short, or Google’s free one, which is more detailed and connects to Google Ads if you ever run them. Either way the site asks permission before it loads anything.

  6. 15

    The financing partner and the real terms

    Partner name, the actual rates and terms, and the pre-qualification link.

    The payment calculator stays off until these are real. A monthly figure on a website is a number a customer will hold you to, so it is not going up as an estimate.

  7. 16

    ctlpro.com or www.ctlpro.com?

    One is the real address and the other forwards to it. Thirty seconds to decide, awkward to change later. Both work for visitors either way.

Block D — Sign-offs

Two reads. One by a lawyer, one by you, and they are checking different things. Both start the day of the session; they are the longest pole in the tent.

  1. 27 Start today

    Your attorney reads four things

    The terms of service, the privacy policy, the six questions on the job application, and the claims section of the storm page. All four were written from scratch to describe what this site genuinely does. They are honest, and they are not legal advice.

    The claims section is the one that matters. Public adjusting in Louisiana is licensed under La. R.S. 22:1691, and the line between documenting damage and negotiating a claim is a legal one. The copy is written to stay on the right side of it, and the hiring questions are written conservatively — every one is about doing the job, the question about ladders and heat describes the work and asks whether you can do it rather than asking about your health, and nothing touches criminal history, age, family or citizenship. But careful is not the same as cleared, and neither of those is a judgement we are qualified to make for you.

  2. 28

    You read the storm and insurance pages yourself

    Separately from the attorney, and for a different reason: do they describe how CTL actually handles a storm job? The first 48 hours, what you do when the adjuster is on site, what you will and will not do during a claim. It is all written from how the business appears to work from the outside. Tell us where it is wrong.

Things left off on purpose

Seven absences that look like gaps and are not. Each one is a decision, and each one is reversible the moment the underlying fact changes.

No review stars in search markup

Google stopped showing star ratings for businesses that mark up reviews about themselves on their own site back in 2019. Adding the code anyway does not produce stars — it risks a penalty across the whole domain. Your stars in search come from the Google Business Profile, which already works.

No monthly payment figures

The estimator exists and is switched off until the financing terms are real ones. A number a customer reads on your site is a number they will expect at signing.

No individual town pages

Sixteen towns are listed on one areas page rather than spread across sixteen near-identical pages. Thin town pages with nothing local on them are the pattern search engines demote. They become worth building once there are case studies to put on them.

No certification badges

Nothing is claimed that has not been confirmed — no FORTIFIED badge, no manufacturer certifications, no licence number. Empty is recoverable. Wrong is not.

No Facebook review feed

Meta shut off the recommendations API entirely in September 2025 — there is no longer any way to pull them automatically, and scraping the page breaks their terms. So the ten on the site were copied by hand and are dated as such. Refreshing them stays a manual job.

No résumé upload yet

The careers page is live and the application form is finished and tested, but until item 10 gives a résumé somewhere to go, the form refuses and points applicants at the office email. A form that accepts a résumé and quietly discards it is worse than no form: the applicant believes they applied, and nobody ever tells them otherwise. The same rule governs the contact form, which refuses to submit rather than losing an enquiry.

No cookie tracking

Analytics are cookieless and only load if the visitor agrees. That keeps the consent banner honest and the privacy policy short.

How it runs

The site is a set of static files — no database, nothing to patch on a Tuesday. It is designed to be hosted on Cloudflare Pages, which serves it free at the traffic a regional contractor sees, and it loads fast because there is very little to load.

One exception, and it is worth knowing about. A file cannot be sent to a set of static files, so the résumé upload on the careers page needs something running behind it. That piece is written and tested — it takes an application, checks it hard, and puts the file in private storage only you can reach — and it is the only moving part on the whole site. It is also the one piece we would rather hand to somebody else, which is what item 10 decides.

Content lives in plain, structured files rather than being buried in the page code, which means the reviews, the town list, the services and the gallery can all be edited without touching the design. If CTL later wants a proper editing interface, that plugs into the same files.

Running cost is close to nothing — hosting is free at this scale, the form service has a free tier, and the only per-visitor cost is the Google reviews lookup, which is worth watching for the first week and then forgetting about.

The current site is untouched. No DNS has been pointed anywhere and ctlpro.com is running exactly as it was. Going live on your own domain is a deliberate, scheduled step with its own order of operations — set out under “what happens after the session” below — and it does not happen before item 02 is in hand.

What happens after the session

So the timeline is clear before anyone commits to a date. The switch itself takes minutes; the two weeks before it are what make those minutes uneventful.

  1. Same day — the attorney starts reading, Business Profile ownership gets confirmed, and we take a full copy of the current site’s pages and settings while it is still up.
  2. Week one — every account and key from Blocks A and B gets wired in. The site goes onto a private test address with everything switched on, and we work through it properly: submit the real form, book a real appointment, apply for a real job, and confirm each one arrives in the right inbox and the right list.
  3. Week two — you review it on that test address. Content from Block C goes in as it arrives. Legal comes back and gets applied.
  4. The switch — your email settings are recreated and verified first, then the address moves. We send and receive a test email on ctlpro.com before calling it done. Old page addresses forward to their new homes so nothing you have ever linked to breaks.
  5. The week after — Google is told about the new site, we watch for anything broken, and whoever is maintaining the gallery gets walked through it. The old site stays paid up for a month, so going back stays a five-minute job if it is ever needed.

The short version

Bring to the session

The registrar login. Whoever hosts the site now, so we can ask them for the settings export. The Google account that owns your Business Profile. Your contractor licence number. Your attorney’s details. CTL’s legal name and tax number for the accounts we set up together.

Decide in the session

Where résumés go. What you use to track customers. Which inbox gets leads, and who else sees it. Call tracking, yes or no. Which statistics. The financing partner. And whether the address is ctlpro.com or www.ctlpro.com.

Two that stop it working

Only two items genuinely prevent the site doing its job: a form key so the contact form can send, and somewhere for résumés to go. Everything else degrades gracefully — a section hides itself rather than showing a gap, which is why nothing on this site reads as unfinished.

One that can go wrong quietly

The DNS settings from your current host. It is the difference between a launch nobody notices and a morning where company email has stopped and nobody knows why. A five-minute request, and it needs making before anything moves.
